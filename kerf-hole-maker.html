Kerf Hole Maker

A grid of test holes stepping around one target size. Push the actual dowel, bolt or bearing into each and find the one it grips — that hole size is the one to draw. Two rows, so it barely costs any board.

What this is for. A laser hole comes out wider than the circle you drew, by roughly the beam width — the kerf. Draw a 6 mm hole and a 6 mm dowel rattles in it. This tool cuts your target size plus five smaller and four larger. Try the real part in each: for a press fit (dowel, bearing housing) take the smallest hole it seats into by hand; for a clearance fit (bolt passing through) take the smallest hole it drops through cleanly. Draw that size on the real job and it fits first time. Roughly, kerf = part Ø − drawn hole that grips.

How to use it

Cut the tester

  1. Engrave the JPG on the board you'll build from, at your usual settings.
  2. Cut the SVG over it without moving the board.
  3. Take the real dowel, bolt or bearing and try it in each hole.

Read it

  • Dowel / glue-up: the smallest hole the dowel pushes into snugly by hand. Glue swells timber, so leave it just shy of a hammer fit.
  • Bearing: the smallest hole the outer race seats into with firm thumb pressure or one light tap, no side-to-side wobble.
  • Bolt clearance: the smallest hole the bolt drops through freely. Go one step up if you want the head to self-centre.

Why the drawn size isn't the part size

The beam eats a little material all the way round the circle, so the finished hole is larger than the line — usually by 0.1–0.25 mm on the diameter depending on machine, power, speed and board. Read the engraved number under the hole that fit, draw that on the real job, and note it on the wall beside your comb figure. Recut this whenever the board or settings change.
